A paper submitted to arXiv on 15 November 2026 proposes the Formal Context Protocol (FCP) for checking artifacts generated by large language models against the requests that prompted them. Its author, Shashank, argues that tests written by the same model as an artifact can share a misunderstanding of the request and therefore provide weak evidence when they pass.[^1]

FCP separates two questions. The first is **conformance**: whether an artifact satisfies a formal reading of a requirement, which the abstract says model checkers and satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) solvers decide. The second is **fidelity**: whether that reading captures what the original prose means. The abstract says fidelity has no decision procedure, though a concrete witness can refute a reading. It describes mechanical checks for eight ways a proof can falsely certify a requirement, with unresolved questions put to the request's author.[^1]

The abstract reports results for four hardware designs and ten code tasks. It says AI-written assertions certified 23% of violated hardware requirements, while FCP certified none. For the code tasks, it reports that code from three language models passed its own tests while violating the request, and that FCP certified three of 33 violations. These are the paper's reported findings; the abstract alone does not establish how the experiments were conducted or whether others can reproduce them.[^1]

:::analysis
The claim sits at **T4, outputs**: a verifier needs evidence that an artifact meets a formal requirement, while the interpretation of the original request remains a separate trust question. Users and operators should examine the formal reading, any witnesses and questions returned, and how their own checking setup handles them. Identifiability also matters: if the exact version and configuration serving a user are recorded and checkable, that user can determine whether they were exposed to an identified issue; the abstract does not identify an affected or fixed version for FCP.[^1]
:::
